How pieces are made

The way the lettering is finished gives each piece a different character. These cross-section diagrams introduce four principal techniques.

PREPARING THE WOOD

Preparing and sanding the wood

Good surface preparation comes before carving.

Purchased wooden blanks should first be checked for flatness and surface condition, then sanded as needed before applying lettering templates and carving.

Angular abrasive grains cut and scrape away small surface peaks. Coarser grains suit initial shaping but leave deeper scratches; finer grains refine the surface in later stages.

The maker’s supplied notes describe #400 wet-and-dry abrasive paper for levelling and recommend #800 for wet sanding after removing a lettering template. The choice depends on the wood, lacquer layers, and stage of work. Moisture should be controlled to avoid excessive water absorption by the wood.

The labelled dimensions, approximately 17–24 μm, refer to individual grains, not the average of the entire abrasive. The original notes list about 35 μm for #400 and 21.8 μm for #800. These are values from the supplied material; grading systems and products differ, so “#800” is not a universal grain size.

01

Written pieces

Characters are brushed in lacquer directly onto the wood, preserving the expression of the brush.

02

Carved pieces

Lettering is cut into the wood and lacquer is applied within the cuts, creating outlines and shadows.

03

Filled-carved pieces

Incised characters are filled with lacquer-based material and polished level with the wood for a smooth finish.

04

Raised-lacquer pieces

After filling and polishing, additional lacquer is built up over the lettering to create raised characters.

LACQUER & LIGHT

Why does lacquer look glossy or matte?

The key is where reflected light goes, rather than simply how much light is reflected.

SurfaceReflectionAppearance
Smoother surfaceNearby surface regions face similar directions, concentrating reflected light near the specular direction.Bright highlights, and sometimes a recognizable reflection of the light source, create a glossy appearance.
Microscopically uneven surfaceDifferent local slopes spread reflected light across a wider range of directions: diffuse reflection or scattering.Highlights become less concentrated and reflections less distinct, producing a matte appearance.

Each small surface region still approximately follows the law of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. A rough surface acts like many tiny mirrors facing different directions. Matte surfaces do reflect light; they distribute it more broadly. Real lacquer surfaces generally combine specular and scattered reflection.

In black lacquer, some light entering the coating is absorbed. Light reflected at its surface can still form bright highlights, so a black finish can also be glossy.

Lighting, viewing angle, and surface microstructure all affect appearance. Gloss and matte are different visual qualities; neither alone determines craftsmanship or establishes that a surface causes eye fatigue.

Reading the original illustrations

  • The supplied illustrations retain their original Japanese wording. “Complete reflection” should be read as a concentration of reflected light near the specular direction, not reflection of all incident light.
  • “Complete diffuse reflection” should be understood as microscopic unevenness spreading reflected light across more directions. A specular component may remain.
  • The scale bars differ: 30 μm and 10 μm. Apparent feature sizes in the two images cannot be compared directly as a measure of roughness. The images illustrate the concept but do not by themselves quantify a gloss difference.
  • The original references to a “premium feel” express aesthetic preference. The claim about eye fatigue cannot be established from these images alone.
